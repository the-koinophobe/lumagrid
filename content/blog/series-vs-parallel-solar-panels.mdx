When an installer fixes your panels, they connect them in **series**, **parallel**, or a mix. This changes how power reaches your inverter.

## Series: like a chain

Connect the plus of one panel to the minus of the next.

- **Voltage adds up.**
- **Current stays the same.**

Example: 4 panels of about 40V each in series ≈ 160V.

## Parallel: side by side

Connect all pluses together and all minuses together.

- **Current adds up.**
- **Voltage stays the same.**

Example: 4 panels of about 13A in parallel ≈ 52A at 40V.

## Side by side

<div className="table-scroll">

| | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage | Adds up | Stays the same |
| Current | Stays the same | Adds up |
| Cable thickness | Thinner | Thicker |
| Best with | MPPT inverters | Low-voltage controllers |
| Shade | One shaded panel affects the string | Less effect on others |

</div>

## Why most home systems use series

Modern hybrid inverters with MPPT want a higher voltage. Series wiring gives that. Lower current means thinner, cheaper cables and less power lost on long runs, like on an [upstairs building](/blog/solar-on-upstairs-building).

## When parallel helps

If some panels face a different direction, or one part of the roof gets shade, two separate strings in parallel can help. Many inverters also have **two MPPT inputs** for this.

## The voltage limit

Every inverter has a maximum input voltage. Panel voltage rises on cool mornings. Too many panels in series can go over the limit and damage the inverter. A good installer calculates this. Read [how many panels for a 5kVA inverter](/blog/how-many-solar-panels-for-5kva-inverter).
